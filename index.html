FrontEditor’s Message / 03
Dear Reader / A Letter from the Desk

The quiet kind

We usually recognize misogyny when it is loud - harassment, violence, abuse, openly hostile language. This issue is about the other register.

What about the advice that tells a girl how to dress? The expectation that a wife should sacrifice more? The assumption that a mother should put everyone before herself? The judgment of a woman who speaks too loudly, stays out too late, chooses not to marry, or prioritizes her career?

These practices may not always appear discriminatory. Sometimes they arrive dressed as tradition, protection, respectability, responsibility - or simply “the way things are.”

This magazine asks what happens when such expectations are repeated so often that they become difficult to recognize as social constructions at all.

More importantly, we examine a difficult question: can women themselves reproduce misogynistic norms? Our answer is yes - but this does not mean blaming women for patriarchy. It means examining how social norms are learned, internalized, negotiated and passed from one generation to another.

Through five theoretical perspectives, historical context, contemporary cases, interviews and critical reflection, The Other Margin looks at the misogyny that is visible - and the misogyny that ordinary life teaches us to overlook.

FoundationsDefinitions / 04
Misogyny is more than “hating women”

A working definition

The dictionary will tell you misogyny means hatred or contempt toward women. Our publication uses a broader diagnostic understanding: misogyny operates through hostility, devaluation, policing and punishment directed at women because they are women - especially when they challenge gendered expectations.

Loud: Direct Hostility

Street harassment & stalking

Direct physical violence

Overt sexist insults & threats

Explicit workplace discrimination

Quiet: Normative Policing

Judging volume, tone & laughter

Policing modesty & clothing

Guilt for prioritizing career

Presumption that a wife must adjust

Structural: The Baseline

Unequal inheritance division

Uncompensated domestic care

Curfews framed as safety

Family headship default to men

Theoretical Anchor / Kate Manne (2017)

In Down Girl: The Logic of Misogyny, philosopher Kate Manne argues that misogyny is not a psychological hostility held in individual men's heads. It is the law-enforcement branch of patriarchy. Its primary purpose is not to hate all women indiscriminately, but to police the boundary: rewarding women who comply with subordination, and punishing women who claim equal authority, autonomy, or voice.

The quiet column is longer. That is the point.

Diagnostic Prompt

When someone criticizes a woman's tone, clothing, or ambitions, ask: Is this a neutral observation, or is social judgment enforcing an unwritten rule?

Etymology: Greek mīsos (hatred) + gunē (woman). In modern sociology, broadened from private sentiment to systematic social regulation.

FoundationsThree Concepts / 05
Three concepts we must separate

Not the same thing

These three words get used interchangeably in casual discussion. They shouldn't be. Each names a completely different level of social organization.

01 / SYSTEM

Patriarchy

The overarching social architecture of gendered power.

It organizes inheritance, lineage, family headship, and institutional authority. It predates any living individual and outlives individual goodwill.

02 / IDEOLOGY

Sexism

The cultural beliefs and stereotypes that justify inequality.

Operates through idioms, jokes, and proverbs that frame men as natural leaders and women as natural caretakers. It can be voiced affectionately and still constrain lives.

03 / ENFORCEMENT

Misogyny

The disciplinary mechanism that polices and punishes violations.

The active hostility, shaming, or social penalty applied when a woman steps outside prescribed boundaries or refuses subordination.

FoundationsPerceive / Overlook / 06
What we perceive - and what we overlook

Ordinary is not
the same as harmless

We immediately recognize the loud register: physical violence, overt sexual harassment, hostile threats, and workplace exclusion. Society agrees these are injustices.

But listen to what circulates without a legal code attached. Each of the sentences below may sound ordinary in a Dhaka drawing room. Ordinary does not mean harmless: it usually means thoroughly rehearsed.

“Good girls don’t behave like that.”

“A woman should know how to adjust.”

“Why was she outside so late?”

“A married woman should prioritize her family over herself.”

“Girls shouldn’t talk or laugh so loudly in public.”

“Women should dress respectfully if they want safety.”

“She is too ambitious for her own good.”

Diagnostic Inquiries

Who is always expected to adjust? Who defines what is "respectable"? Would the same rule ever apply to a man? What exact social price does a woman pay when she refuses?

MechanismsThe Cycle / 07
How does misogyny reproduce itself?

The wheel nobody steers

01 / SOCIALIZATION

A child learns assigned gender expectations from family.

02 / REPETITION

Media, schooling, peers and relatives repeat the scripts.

03 / NORMALIZATION

The arbitrary standard begins feeling natural and timeless.

04 / INTERNALIZATION

The woman monitors herself to preempt community judgment.

05 / LATERAL POLICING

Family members and peers judge anyone who steps out of line.

06 / TRANSMISSION

The standard is taught to the next generation: the cycle restarts.

The norm
enforces
itself

The central sociological insight:

A social norm becomes indestructible when it no longer requires external force, because people begin enforcing it on themselves.

Once a cycle reaches self-enforcement, no central conspirator is required. Each turn of the wheel arrives disguised as common sense, good parenting, or moral responsibility.

Theoretical Connection / Michel Foucault

Disciplinary power does not rely solely on direct violence. It operates through continuous surveillance, normalizing judgment, and self-regulation. See detailed examination on page 11.

Break any single spoke in this wheel, and the entire momentum slows down.

Theoretical Framework / Analytical Foundations

Five lenses, one problem

Instead of five disconnected biographies, five diagnostic instruments. Each thinker hands us a different analytical magnification to examine everyday life in Bangladesh.

LENS 01 / ONTOLOGY

Simone de Beauvoir (1949)

Deconstructs how woman is defined only in relation to man: as the 'Other' rather than an autonomous subject.

LENS 02 / STRUCTURE

“How does gender organize power?”

Analyzes the gender order and hegemonic masculinity, demonstrating how cultural dominance rewards male authority.

LENS 03 / DISCIPLINE

Michel Foucault (1975)

“How do we learn to regulate ourselves?”

Reveals panoptic surveillance: power moving into bodily posture, modesty codes, and internalized self-policing.

LENS 04 / DISCOURSE

Stuart Hall (1997)

“How do representations manufacture reality?”

Examines how media archetypes (the self-sacrificing wife, the fairness imperative) encode and naturalize dominant ideology.

LENS 05 / STRATEGY

Deniz Kandiyoti (1988)

“Why might women negotiate with constraint?”

Introduces the 'patriarchal bargain', explaining why women often accommodate restrictive systems to secure domestic safety, social protection, and family standing.

Simone de Beauvoir / 1949Woman as the “Other”
B
Beauvoir’s analysis examines how woman is defined as relative to a male “Self” - rather than treated first and foremost as an autonomous subject.

In everyday life, this appears whenever a woman is introduced through her relations instead of herself: the good daughter, the good wife, the good mother. Each phrase contains a hidden preposition - good to whom?

The question becomes: is she being recognized for who she is - or for how well she performs the identity society has assigned to her?

Theory / Lens 04Hall: Representation in Media / 12
Stuart Hall / 1997The woman we keep seeing
H
Representation is not simply about showing reality. It helps construct the conceptual systems through which reality is understood. Hall examines how images, language and cultural codes operate as signifying practices - repetition doing the ideological work of proof.
Case Study 01 / Television Drama

The "Lokkhi Bou" Archetype

In classic Bangladeshi television serials and telefilms, virtuous womanhood is systematically encoded as self-effacing domestic service, emotional restraint, and silent endurance. Outspoken or ambitious women are framed as disruptive to family harmony. Through decades of primetime repetition, this trope naturalizes female compliance as moral virtue.

Case Study 02 / Commercial Media

The Fairness Imperative

Billboard and broadcast advertising across Dhaka markets skin lightening as a prerequisite for self-worth, respectability, and career advancement. Drawing on Hall’s "Spectacle of the Other", dark skin is encoded as a personal lack to be remedied, while fairness is encoded as social mobility. Commercial media does not merely sell cream; it manufactures racialized gender norms.

Encoding & Decoding / Stuart Hall

Media texts encode dominant cultural ideologies. When audiences consume these images without critical resistance, they adopt the "preferred reading" - accepting that quiet, self-sacrificing behavior is what a woman naturally is, rather than what culture repeatedly demands she become.

Core Takeaway

A representation does not need to lie. It only needs to repeat until cultural construction is mistaken for nature.

Deniz Kandiyoti / 1988The patriarchal bargain
K
Kandiyoti argues that women develop strategies within concrete patriarchal constraints. She calls these strategies “patriarchal bargains.” Different systems create different “rules of the game,” and women negotiate those rules to maximize security or life options.
What conformity can buy
family acceptance
security
social status
economic protection
reduced conflict

Does conformity always mean agreement?

Not necessarily. A woman may follow a traditional expectation because it steadies her life - while privately finding the rules unjust.

Therefore: accommodation can be a strategy for survival, not proof that oppression is freely accepted. This is why we should be careful before judging women who conform to patriarchal expectations. The bargain is made under conditions the bargainer did not set.

Ask yourself

Whose consent is easiest to collect? The one who agrees - or the one for whom refusal is most expensive?

This lens will do heavy work on page 24, when we ask whether women are part of the problem.

Theory / SynthesisFive Lenses, One Life / 14
Applied Theory / Synthesis Across Five Frameworks

One woman, five theories

Scenario: A married woman in Dhaka continues her corporate career while expecting shared domestic labor. Watch what each analytical framework illuminates.

01 / Beauvoir

Relational Construction: Her husband's career is framed as standard breadwinning; hers is framed as an optional luxury that must never compromise domestic duties. When introduced at family gatherings, her professional credentials are overshadowed by whether she is a "cooperative daughter-in-law" (lokkhi bou).

02 / Connell

The Gender Order: Relatives warn the husband that sharing domestic chores diminishes his authority within the kinship network. Hegemonic masculinity disciplines both: she is penalized for ambition, while he is teased for being "controlled" if he cooks or does laundry.

03 / Foucault

Panoptic Self-Surveillance: Nobody explicitly commands her to leave the office early. Yet she glances at her watch at 5:30 PM with acute anxiety, skips professional networking events, and perpetually apologizes for working late. The community gaze has entered her conscience.

04 / Hall

Representational Coding: Primetime television serials have trained everyone around her to associate career-oriented wives with familial neglect and conflict. Even when she manages both domains brilliantly, family members interpret her career through the pre-packaged media trope of the "selfish modern woman."

05 / Kandiyoti

The Patriarchal Bargain: To neutralize family resistance and protect her career, she strikes an exhausting bargain: waking at 5:00 AM to cook meals before leaving for work, over-compensating domestically to purchase peace and legitimacy for her professional autonomy.

Core Synthesis Conclusion

One ordinary life decision contains identity construction, structural inequality, bodily surveillance, media coding, and strategic survival - operating at the exact same moment.

When we understand these five forces together, we stop asking naive questions like "Why doesn't she just assert herself?" and begin seeing the structural matrix she must navigate every morning.

When women police women

The 2025 controversy involving Bangladeshi model Tangia Zaman Methila shows misogynistic objectification circulating inside online audiences - including from women.

Do not make this argument

“Women are the real problem.”

A lazy conclusion that flatters the structure by blaming its casualties.

A woman can participate in: sexual objectification / body shaming / slut-shaming / appearance policing / moral judgment. Misogynistic norms can circulate between women as well as between men and women.

ColumnProtection / Control / 17
Special column

Protection,
or control?

Plate 05 - The grill keeps things out. It also keeps things in.

A parent says: “I’m restricting you for your own safety.”

We should not immediately dismiss the concern. Women genuinely face safety risks, and love often stands behind the lock on the door.

But watch what happens to the question being asked. Instead of “How can we make public spaces safer?” it becomes “Why was she outside?” Instead of “How do we prevent harassment?” it becomes “Why was she dressed like that?”

The risk is real - but the remedy moves, quietly, from the conditions that produce insecurity to the woman who must live with it.

Our critical question

When does protection become control?

Not every protective action is misogynistic. Protection becomes problematic when women’s freedom is disproportionately restricted while the underlying conditions producing insecurity remain unchallenged.

The Other Margin17 / 32
ColumnClothing & Agency / 18
Bodily Autonomy / Moving Beyond the Liberal-Conservative Binary

Who gets to choose what a woman wears?

The real question is never the garment itself. The real question is agency: who decides, who enforces the boundary, and what exact social price follows refusal.

01 / Voluntary Hijab

Agency: A religious, cultural, or personal conviction that belongs entirely to the woman. When chosen freely, the garment represents an act of self-determination and personal dignity.

02 / Forced Confinement

Coercion: When family members or local institutions mandate dress under threat of punishment, genuine autonomy is abolished regardless of the garment's religious meaning to others.

03 / Community Shaming

Street Surveillance: When a woman walking through Dhaka is harassed, slandered, or denied safety for wearing jeans or unpinned dupattas, social policing treats her body as public property.

04 / Elite Mockery

Exclusionary Bias: When professional or academic spaces subtly diminish a woman's intellect because she wears a burqa or hijab, the pressure is equally coercive and paternalistic.

The Double-Bind in Urban Bangladesh

Women across Bangladesh navigate an impossible tightrope: criticized if their clothing is perceived as "overly Western" or modern, and simultaneously dismissed if they wear traditional religious attire. This reveals the core mechanism: society claims jurisdiction over women's appearance, turning dress into an instrument of moral judgment rather than personal freedom.

Autonomy is not about looking modern. It is about having the right to decide without fear.

Analytical Rule

Avoid the simplistic formula where traditional equals oppressed and modern equals liberated. Freedom cannot be measured by the hem of a garment; it can only be measured by whether a woman had real sovereignty to choose.

ColumnLeadership / 19
Political Authority / The South Asian Paradox

Can women lead without being judged as women?

Bangladesh presents a globally recognized political paradox: women have occupied the Prime Ministership for over three decades, yet ordinary women remain severely marginalized across local governance and corporate leadership.

Apex Power vs Grassroots Reality

Dynastic political succession has historically allowed exceptional women to command state authority in South Asia. However, symbolic representation at the summit frequently acts as an alibi: institutions point to female national leaders to deny that systemic patriarchy persists in corporate boardrooms, union halls, and university faculties.

A male leader showing firmness is praised as decisive and strong.

A female leader showing the same firmness is branded shrill, vindictive, or emotional.

The Double Standard Checklist

Notice which categories of scrutiny attach exclusively to women in public life:

vocal tone marital status childcare duties clothing choices emotional demeanor physical age laugh volume

Connell's gender order and Hall's media framing intersect here: legitimate policy critique is displaced by gendered personal devaluation.

Legitimate Critique vs Gendered Policing

Holding a public official accountable for fiscal policy, human rights, or administrative competence is essential democratic practice. Questioning her right to exercise authority because of her gender, marital state, or appearance is misogyny wearing the mask of politics.

Core Diagnostic

“Would this exact criticism ever be leveled against a man in the same position?”

Column / The Other Margin19 / 32
Discourse & RepresentationBody Politics & Cultural Capital / 20
Sovereignty, Spectatorship, and the Commercial Gaze

Who owns a woman’s image?

When a woman asserts herself in public culture, society immediately divides her image into competing moral and commercial claims. The inquiry is neither puritanical nor consumerist: it examines who exercises sovereignty over the meaning of her visibility.

TENSION I / SUBJECTHOOD

Agency vs The Gaze

Does the creative act originate from internal aesthetic desire, or is it structured by an anticipatory compliance with what an external spectator rewards? Autonomy requires that the subject defines her own exposure rather than conforming to an audience's appetite.

TENSION II / SEMIOTICS

Expression vs Sexualization

Audiences frequently project sexualization onto images intended as art, athletic discipline, or fashion sovereignty. Society often holds the creator guilty for the voyeuristic gaze of the viewer, conflating the woman's presentation with the viewer's consumption.

TENSION III / MATERIALITY

Choice vs Precarity

In a digital attention economy, visibility converts directly into livelihood. When cultural industries systematically penalize quiet craft and over-reward hyper-visible bodily performance, individual choice is heavily disciplined by material survival and platform incentives.

Methodological Lens: Four Sites of Image Politics
SITE OF PRODUCTION

Who held the camera, drafted the contract, funded the project, and retained final editorial cut? Power resides in the conditions of production.

SITE OF THE IMAGE

What formal visual codes, lighting techniques, and postures are mobilized? Does the framing treat the body as an active agent or a static ornament?

SITE OF CIRCULATION

How do social platform algorithms extract engagement from moral outrage? Algorithmic systems weaponize women's visibility to maximize clicks and commentary.

SITE OF AUDIENCING

Does the spectator encounter the subject with relational respect or punitive moralism? Audiences actively decode images through their own ideological filters.

A woman’s visibility does not automatically signify exploitation, nor does an audience’s voyeurism prove that her expression lacked agency. True sovereignty begins when we stop treating a woman’s public appearance as a community property subject to collective moral policing.

Structural ReproductionThe Social Ecosystem / 22
Institutional Synergy and the Architecture of Persistence

The self-reproducing ecosystem

Misogyny rarely survives through a single isolated institution. It endures because domestic socialization, peer surveillance, media narratives, and economic precarity form a continuous circuit where each pressure reinforces the next until compliance feels like ordinary common sense.

Eight Structural Vectors of Reinforcement
DOMESTIC SOCIALIZATION

Differential upbringing in the home: boys are raised for outward entitlement, while girls are trained for emotional management and unpaid domestic care.

PEER SURVEILLANCE

Immediate social circles punish non-conformity through whispering, reputational marking, and casual conversational micro-policing.

CULTURAL AXIOMS

Proverbial lore and selective traditions naturalize subordination, recasting historical power imbalances as timeless spiritual or biological mandates.

MEDIA ARCHETYPES

Screen narratives glorify submissive, suffering heroines while coding independent, assertive, or unmarried women as selfish or morally fallen.

MORAL ANXIETY

The persistent terror of becoming "unmarriageable" or compromising family honor operates as an internalized disciplinary warden.

MATERIAL PRECARITY

Unequal inheritance practices, wage gaps, and informal domestic burdens sharply curtail women's capacity to walk away from hostile arrangements.

LEGITIMACY BARGAINS

Women gain social standing, protection, and matriarchal security by proving themselves the most rigorous custodians of conventional decorum.

INTERNALIZED CONTROL

External censure becomes automated self-censorship: the subject preemptively curtails her own voice, gait, and desires before any outside rebuke occurs.

THE HYDRAULIC OF NORMALIZATION

These are not isolated causes; they are mutually compensating valves. If a woman achieves material independence, moral anxiety and family honor intensify to preserve the status quo.

The Complex Question of Female Complicity

The uncomfortable question

Do women participate in the reproduction of misogyny? Yes - but mistaking transmission for authorship is a fatal analytical error. When women police other women, they are operating within severe survival parameters dictated by the patriarchal structure.

The Transmission Chain: How Discipline Migrates
VULNERABILITY & EXPOSURE

A young woman experiences early social policing and witnesses the swift social ostracism inflicted upon women who breach collective boundaries.

PRAGMATIC ACCOMMODATION

Recognizing that open rebellion carries severe economic and reputational penalties, she adopts conventional modesty as a rational risk-mitigation strategy.

LATERAL SURVEILLANCE

She enforces identical restrictions onto her daughters, sisters, and peers, sincerely believing she is shielding them from community condemnation.

STATUS EXTRACTION

Society confers moral authority and domestic standing onto women who prove themselves to be the most rigorous custodians of traditional decorum.

INTERGENERATIONAL PERSISTENCE

The hierarchy reproduces itself across generations without requiring constant male intervention: the system operates through self-administering subjects.

THE CATEGORY ERROR: CONDUIT VS ARCHITECT

Blaming women for sustaining patriarchal norms confuses the conduit with the architect. A guard may lock the cell doors, but she did not draft the penal code, build the penitentiary, or profit from the confinement. Attributing misogyny to women’s choices exonerates the institutional framework.

Deniz Kandiyoti: The Classic Bargain

“In patrilocal extended households, women enter with zero institutional leverage. Their lifetime security depends on producing sons and safeguarding family honor. The mother-in-law's authority over junior women is the singular systemic dividend promised to her for enduring decades of subordination.”

“Victim and participant at the same time: the system recruits its own constrained subjects as its frontline inspectors, punishing them doubly if they refuse the post.”

A Rigorous Academic Project Must Challenge Its Own Boundaries

Are we calling everything misogyny?

Critical inquiry risks intellectual obsolescence if it turns into totalizing dogma. If every social friction, interpersonal disagreement, or cultural distinction is automatically categorized as misogyny, the concept loses diagnostic precision and political credibility.

CONCEPT DILUTION

The Risk of Over-Expansion

When every gender distinction is labeled misogyny, we lose the vocabulary to identify lethal patriarchal violence and systemic economic disenfranchisement.

RESPONSIBILITY SHIFT

The Hazard of Lateral Blame

Documenting female complicity can easily be hijacked by conservative forces to displace structural accountability away from male-dominated hierarchies.

AGENCY REDUCTION

False Consciousness Fallacy

Assuming any woman choosing a domestic or faith-rooted life is blindly brainwashed is condescending and dismisses conscious female navigation.

CULTURAL REDUCTION

Imported Dogmatism

Superimposing metropolitan frameworks onto rural or working-class realities without understanding kinship reciprocity leads to disconnected elitism.

Diagnostic Matrix: Misogyny vs Contextual Social Negotiation
DIMENSION CONTEXTUAL SOCIAL NEGOTIATION STRUCTURAL MISOGYNY
Enforcement Mode Voluntary participation with safe room for refusal, dissent, or revision. Enforced through fear, reputational lynching, economic cutoff, or physical sanction.
Reciprocity & Leverage Bilateral expectations where care and obligation are mutually shared. Unilateral extraction where female self-erasure is required to maintain male status.
Moral Axiom Affectionate tradition adaptable to contemporary individual dignity. Essentialist doctrine declaring female autonomy to be inherently dangerous or evil.
Our Editorial Commitment

We do not argue that every cultural custom is patriarchal. We analyze women’s participation in reproducing norms without treating women as responsible for creating the structural conditions in which those norms operate. Maintaining this distinction protects critical inquiry from degenerating into moralistic posturing.

Transformative PraxisStrategic Interventions & Structural Reform / 26
From Individual Anxiety to Collective Reconfiguration

Breaking the cycle

Dismantling everyday misogyny cannot rely on moral lecturing alone. Because the cycle operates across domestic, cultural, institutional, and economic domains, transformation requires coordinated structural disruption at every point of reproduction.

DOMESTIC PEDAGOGY
Household & Upbringing

Abolishing gendered chore allocation. Conditioning boys to perform emotional care and domestic labor, while teaching girls that assertiveness, anger, and intellectual independence are inherent human rights.

CRITICAL SEMIOTICS
Cultural & Media Literacy

Equipping youth to decode media archetypes. Actively questioning screen narratives that romanticize female suffering and challenging algorithmic networks that profit from moral sensationalism.

INSTITUTIONAL SHIELDING
Campuses & Workplaces

Establishing genuinely independent, confidential grievance bodies. Removing harassment cases from informal family mediation into formal institutional justice, eliminating career and academic reprisals.

SOLIDARITY NETWORKS
Peer Groups & Neighborhoods

Refusing to participate in whisper networks and reputational policing. Creating protective social buffers around women who challenge community conventions to absorb the shock of family censure.

MATERIAL AUTONOMY
Economic & Property Rights

Securing equitable property inheritance, closing wage disparities, and establishing affordable childcare. Without material independence, intellectual awareness cannot shield a woman from coercive arrangements.

Every intervention above dismantles one quadrant of the operational cycle from page 07. Individual courage begins the rupture, but organized institutional solidarity is what makes freedom durable.

Final ReflectionThe Legible and the Submerged / 30
The Dual Architecture of Misogyny in Everyday Bangladesh

What we perceive / what we overlook

Public outrage fixates on spectacular eruptions of patriarchal hostility, while remaining blind to the dense, ambient infrastructure of daily discipline that quietly prepares the ground for them.

THE LEGIBLE SPHERE
What We Perceive
PHYSICAL VIOLENCE & ASSAULT

The acute bodily violation that registers in police dockets, emergency rooms, and sensational news headlines.

AGGRESSIVE STREET HARASSMENT

The hostile catcalling, stalking, and physical intimidation that produces acute public fear and panic.

VIRAL DIGITAL SMEARING

The coordinated online defamation campaigns designed to destroy a woman’s public reputation overnight.

INSTITUTIONAL DENIAL

The overt exclusion from hiring, leadership promotions, and organizational decision-making recorded on paper.

THE SUBMERGED INFRASTRUCTURE
What We Overlook
UNSOLICITED MORAL INSTRUCTION

“Walk softer,” “Lower your gaze,” “Do not speak loudly” - discipline naturalized as loving family guidance.

CONFINEMENT AS "PROTECTION"

Restricting a daughter's mobility and educational choices rather than making public streets safe for women.

THE GOOD WOMAN PEDESTAL

Sacralizing female self-erasure while coding ambitious, outspoken, or unmarried women as selfish or dangerous.

THE EXHAUSTION OF DOMESTIC CARE

Silently draining women's creative and intellectual energy through unreciprocated domestic and emotional labor.

CLOSING SYNTHESIS / READING THE MARGINS OF EVERYDAY LIFE

The left column is what shocks us; the right column is what we tolerate before breakfast. Misogyny does not begin when a woman is assaulted; it begins years earlier, when a girl is taught that her safety depends on making herself small, quiet, and invisible. Combating only the loud violations while excusing the quiet courtesies is like pruning poisonous leaves while watering the root. The work of this magazine has been to make the invisible margins readable.
